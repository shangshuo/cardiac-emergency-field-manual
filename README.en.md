### This is v1.3 of what began as an emergency manual for sudden cardiac events. Compared with earlier versions, it mainly restructures the content and updates parts of it against the latest evidence-based guidelines and research. Most importantly, the restructuring makes the core of the material far more actionable, so that any ordinary person facing such a situation can respond quickly by following an SOP.

### The reason this series exists: although there is plenty of public education in this area, most of it stops at tool-level skills — how to do CPR, what an AED is, and so on. In real situations things are far messier, and many of the details that actually decide the outcome are never mentioned. Most people freeze in an emergency. A manual that stays closer to reality may help more of them.

### Action Manual

> Note: this document assumes that an acute myocardial infarction / cardiac arrest is in progress, which is why the individual must make a judgment call at the moment it happens.

---

#### What to do when you are alone

1. **Call 120** *(mainland China EMS — use your local emergency number)*
   - In normal daytime hours, the best move is to call someone who knows your situation and is **physically closest** to you (a neighbor, a residential-community staff member), and ask them to call 120 on your behalf and come over as fast as possible;
   - Late at night, or when no one nearby can help, the better option is to **call 110** and ask **110** to contact 120 on your behalf and to dispatch officers from your local police station to reach you quickly. When you call 110, state the following:
     (1) First, say that you may be having a heart attack, that you are alone, and that you may lose consciousness at any moment, and ask 110 to help contact 120;
     (2) Second, give your full address;
2. **Open the door**

   This can be done at the same time as the call. While you are on the phone, open the doors of your home in advance and leave them open, so that the 120 crew can come straight in.
3. Keep your airway and breathing unobstructed
   Loosen your collar and belt, and if necessary take off tight clothing entirely;
4. **Aspirin**
   - If the pain feels like a **strong tearing sensation**, take **neither** **aspirin** **nor** **nitroglycerin**;
   - Take **300 mg** of aspirin quickly;
   - The most common version in China is **100 mg per tablet**; the US version is usually **86 mg per tablet**, and is often enteric-coated. So the **Chinese version** normally means **3 tablets**, the **US version** needs **4 tablets**, and if you have the **25 mg per tablet** version you need at least **12 tablets** in one go. *[Translator's note: the author writes 86 mg for the "US version"; the standard low-dose ("baby") aspirin sold in the United States is 81 mg, so 4 tablets of 81 mg ≈ 324 mg. Always follow your own doctor's instructions and your local product label.]*
   - It must be **chewed** — that is, chewed up and swallowed, not swallowed whole;
5. **Lie on your side**

   After taking the aspirin, lie on the bed or sofa in the **right lateral position** (left arm on top, right arm underneath, legs supported and slightly raised) and wait for the rescuers. If you did not take aspirin, stay in a semi-recumbent position with your back supported and lower body lying flat.
6. Oxygen
   If you feel **short of breath**, or you have a fingertip pulse oximeter or a smart watch/band, check your oxygen saturation. If the result is **SpO2 of 90 or below**, and you have an oxygen canister at home (such as the 1000 ml portable oxygen cans common in high-altitude areas), use slow deep breaths of oxygen to ease the discomfort.
7. Measure your blood pressure
   If you are conscious, you are sure help is on the way, your symptoms are not getting worse, and a blood pressure monitor is within reach, you can measure your own blood pressure at this stage:

   ![Blood pressure measurement posture: upper arm in the top panel, lower limb (ankle) in the bottom panel; the cuff must sit level with the heart](images/blood-pressure-measurement.png)

   - When measuring, move from the **right lateral position** back to a semi-recumbent position (upper body resting against the headboard or sofa back, legs kept flat and supported on the bed or sofa), and do not cross your legs;
   - Pay attention to cuff position: it should be level with your heart;
   - Keep the bottom edge of the cuff about 3 cm from the elbow (3 cm is roughly the width of an adult's index finger and thumb held together);
   - You need to measure **both arms** — left arm and right arm, in either order;
   - Each side needs **at least two measurements**, at least **1 minute apart**;
   - If the difference between two consecutive readings on one arm (mainly the systolic value) is **more than 10 mmHg**, wait at least 1 minute and take a **third** reading; if the difference is still **more than 10 mmHg**, wait at least another minute and take a fourth, a fifth… and so on, until **the last two values** are relatively stable, i.e. **less than 10 mmHg** apart;
   - **Record the time of each measurement, which arm, and the values (systolic/diastolic)** — if you have paper and a pen at hand, writing it down is best;
   - Pay special attention to:
     (1) **A difference between the two arms** of **more than 10 mmHg** — if the ambulance crew arrives, **tell the medical staff immediately**; and if you took aspirin earlier, tell them that at the same time;
     (2) Blood pressure that is too low, e.g. systolic below 100 mmHg;
     (3) Blood pressure that is too high, e.g. systolic above 180 mmHg;
8. Cooling down
   If there is an air conditioner, set it to a low temperature such as 16°C/18°C (61°F/64°F); if you have cooling patches or ice packs, place them on the forehead.
9. Arrhythmia
   - If you wear a newer smart watch with an ECG function, watch for any arrhythmia alerts, or run a reading yourself. If it flags **atrial fibrillation**, **ventricular fibrillation** or **atrial flutter**, note it and tell the ambulance crew first thing when they arrive;
   - Newer electronic blood pressure monitors often include arrhythmia detection too, but unlike an ECG they cannot identify the specific type of arrhythmia. If an arrhythmia alert appears while measuring blood pressure, do the same as above and tell the crew when they arrive.
10. Nitroglycerin
    If you have nitroglycerin with you (spray or sublingual tablets), do **not** use **nitroglycerin** if **any** of the following applies:
    - The difference in blood pressure between the two arms is **more than 10 mmHg**
    - Systolic blood pressure is **below 120 mmHg** — if you have a cardiac history and take related medication, use your usual blood pressure at the same time of day as the baseline; for example, if your usual reading at that time of day is 100/70 mmHg, the current reading should at least not fall below your usual value;
    - Systolic value is **90 mmHg or lower**
    - You have used sildenafil, tadalafil or vardenafil within the past 48 hours;
    - The chest or back pain feels **like a tearing sensation**;

    Only when none of these five conditions applies, **and** **chest pain** is the main symptom, may you use the oral sublingual spray or sublingual tablet as directed to relieve symptoms. Otherwise do not use it.
    The standard nitroglycerin dose is **0.5 mg** sublingually, repeatable **once every 5 minutes**, **3 times maximum** — that is, **3 doses within 15 minutes**. If it still has no effect, do not keep taking more.
    The common original-brand nitroglycerin sublingual tablet is the **0.6 mg** version; the common high-volume versions are usually **0.3 mg per tablet**. When buying, remember and write down which version you have, and follow the instructions when using it, to avoid taking too much (which can cause hypotension, bradycardia and syncope) or too little.

> When you are alone, there is not much you can do. Of everything above, by far the most important and central thing is to **get emergency medical personnel to you fast**. Everything else depends on the circumstances. After that: lie still, **do not move anymore**, and quietly wait for the people coming to help.

#### What to do when two people are present

From here on, the person affected is called the **patient**, and the other person is called the **family member**.

1. Get the patient to **lie still** on the bed or sofa first thing. Apart from the necessary steps that follow — taking medication, measuring blood pressure — the patient should **make no other movement at all**.
2. **Call 120**
   - If the family member is healthy, call **120** as fast as possible and request professional emergency personnel;
   - If the family member has limited mobility or is also at constant health risk, call **110** to request rapid support from local police and community staff. As above, state your full residential address and the sudden cardiac problem;
   - If friends or neighbors who know the situation live close by, call them at the same time. Getting 2 more people there greatly improves the efficiency of the rescue; one of them can go to the nearest junction on the most likely route of the ambulance and wait there, then guide the vehicle in as fast as possible, so that unfamiliarity with the route does not waste time.
3. **CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation)**

   If the patient goes into cardiac arrest, **CPR** must be started immediately.
   The three core elements of CPR are **compression location**, **compression rate** and **compression depth**. The standard protocol has a more scientific and rigorous justification behind it, but for an unskilled, flustered family member, getting these three right is enough.

   - **How to tell**
     The patient has lost consciousness. The family member can try tapping the shoulder and speaking loudly; if the patient is confirmed unresponsive, use the simplest reliable way to check for a pulse: **grip the neck** directly and feel for a **pulse**. If no pulse can be felt, **start** **CPR** **immediately**;
   - **Lay the patient flat**
     Move the patient from a semi-recumbent position to a **flat supine position** immediately;
   - **Clear the airway**
     Take anything out of the mouth — dentures, chewing gum, whatever it is. With no experience, you can try passing one arm under the patient's neck as a fulcrum and pushing the jaw upward with the other hand; viewed from the side, the line from throat to mouth should be straight;
   - **Compression location**
     The precise description is the **lower half of the sternum**. It is commonly described as just below the nipple line, but multiple guidelines and studies have found that landmark unreliable, so use a different approach: run your fingers up along the ribs on both sides until you reach the point where the ribs converge, and go just below it (avoiding the ribs and the xiphoid process) — that is the correct **compression point**. If large buttons or other accessories get in the way, remove them or take off the obstructing clothing;
   - **Compression rate**
     The standard rate and method is **100–120 compressions per minute, with 2 rescue breaths after every 30 compressions** — that is: 30 compressions, 2 breaths, 30 compressions, 2 breaths, and so on in a loop. In practice you must also allow for chest recoil (the chest springing back to its original position after each compression). For easy counting, **2 compressions per second** is the easiest figure to remember. Some people use music to keep the beat (for example the Chinese song *Zui Xuan Min Zu Feng*: "Cang [press] mang de [press] tian [press] ya [press] shi [press] wo de [press] ai [press], mian [press] mian de [press] qing [press] shan [press] jiao xia [press] hua [press] zheng [press] kai [press]"…). Note that this song's tempo is slightly faster than the standard, so adjust the rhythm to allow for recoil. *[Translator's note: English-speaking readers often use the Bee Gees' "Stayin' Alive" (about 103 beats per minute) for the same purpose; it is likewise slightly fast, so use it as a guide, not a metronome.]*
   - **Compression depth**
     The standard depth is **5 cm**, roughly the combined height of an adult woman's **index + middle + ring fingers** held together, or an adult man's **index + middle finger plus half of the ring finger**. Compressing to this depth takes real force, and it may well fracture ribs or the sternum (reported in up to 78% of cases) — but life comes first. Even if you hear what sounds like a fracture, keep compressing. Never stop.
   - **Rescue breaths**
     As noted, the standard is 2 rescue breaths after every 30 compressions: open the patient's mouth, take a large breath and blow into the patient's mouth. If you are uncomfortable with direct contact and have tissue or disinfectant wipes at hand, place one over the patient's mouth and blow through it. The detail here: do not inhale into your lungs and then exhale — just hold air in your mouth and blow it directly into the patient's mouth. If you have one of the 1000 ml portable oxygen cans mentioned earlier, holding it down and spraying into the patient's mouth is even better. If you really are not confident or it is impractical, you can **skip rescue breaths** entirely — just keep up standard chest compressions.
   ![Adult CPR hand position: the heel of one hand on the lower half of the sternum, the other hand gripping it to add force](images/cpr-hand-position.jpg)

   - **Hand position**
     Refer to the illustration above. For adult CPR compressions, the heel of one hand is the main point of force, placed over the lower part of the sternum; the other hand grips the hand underneath to add supporting force and help reach adequate depth. For a child, an adult's single palm may be too large — especially an adult man's — so depending on the child's size you may compress with **three fingers of one hand (index, middle and ring finger) together**.

   - **Body posture**
     The correct standard is arms straight. To reach the required depth and keep up the rate, push down using the weight of your whole body: 2 compressions per second, 5 cm deep. This is close to anaerobic exercise. An ordinary person pushing down with bent arms will lose arm strength within a few minutes, so remember the key point: **push down using the strength of your whole body**. This is also why you should have called others for help earlier — ordinary people rarely have the endurance to keep compressing, so you need at least 2 people taking turns to keep the CPR effective.

     ![CPR body posture: arms straight, pressing vertically down using the weight of the whole body](images/cpr-posture.png)
   - **How long to compress**
     Keep going, without stopping, until the patient's heartbeat returns or the ambulance crew arrives. Depending on who is actually on scene, it is recommended to **switch the compressor every 2 minutes**. If an AED has been obtained before the crew arrives, do 2 minutes of CPR first, then let the AED analyze. If the AED advises that a shock is not appropriate, abandon the AED at once and keep compressing. If the AED advises that defibrillation (a shock) is possible, deliver it; if the patient still has no heartbeat afterwards, remove the AED pads and **continue CPR compressions** — do not stop. That is:
     (1) Only one family member: CPR must not stop. Continue at a rhythm of **30 seconds of compressions + 2 rescue breaths** until the patient's heartbeat returns, the ambulance crew arrives, or support arrives (with or without an AED);
     (2) One family member plus a second helper who arrives without an AED: alternate **2 minutes of CPR each**, until the heartbeat returns or the crew arrives;
     (3) One family member plus a second helper who arrives with an AED: thereafter run the cycle **2 minutes of compressions → AED analysis → shock or no shock → patient still has no heartbeat → 2 more minutes of compressions → AED analysis…** until the heartbeat returns or the crew arrives;
     (4) More people on scene: as above, several people can rotate through the CPR (and AED) cycle until the heartbeat returns or the crew arrives.

> CPR is the most powerful thing you can do before professional help arrives, when an acute myocardial infarction or cardiac arrest occurs. The three core elements — **compression location**, **compression rate**, **compression depth** — must be memorized. If you want to bring someone back, CPR has only two endpoints: **the patient's heartbeat returns**, or **professional emergency personnel arrive**.

4. **AED (automated external defibrillator)**

   As noted in the section on calling 120, one person handling an emergency alone is a stretch; ideally get at least 2 more people there at the same time, one of whom knows the area and can go to meet the ambulance. If you can call one more person, have that person look for an AED on the way over and bring it, in case it is urgently needed.

   Given how much else has to be handled on scene, and that CPR must start instantly if cardiac arrest occurs, if only one family member is present, that person must absolutely never leave to go looking for an AED.

   - **Where to find an AED**
     Search directly for **AED** in a map app on your phone (Amap/Gaode Maps, Apple Maps, etc.). Coverage in large cities is already very high: nearby hospitals, clinics, schools, shopping malls, neighborhood/subdistrict committees and government buildings are usually equipped. Even if the map app cannot guide you, asking at the nearest such place has a very good chance of success. When taking the device from a self-service cabinet or anywhere else, first check the **battery level** — there should be dedicated maintenance, but we cannot rule out the chance that it has sat unused for a long time without a battery change. If there is a battery problem and no spare battery on site, go immediately to the next nearest one; do not waste time, and above all do not deprive the patient of possible use.
   - **Using an AED**
     As in the CPR section above: if the patient has gone into cardiac arrest, do 2 minutes of CPR first, then use the AED. Do not worry about not knowing how: today's AEDs are very smart. The electrode pads carry clear diagrams, and once powered on the device gives full voice guidance (some also show images or even video tutorials on an LCD screen). Whether defibrillation (a shock) is appropriate is decided entirely by the machine's own analysis; the user only needs to press a simple button. The one critical warning: **everyone else must stay at least 1 meter away from the patient**, and must absolutely **not** be in **contact** with the patient or with any potentially conductive object next to the patient.
   - If, after the AED delivers a shock, the patient is still unresponsive and the AED indicates that it is recharging, procedures differ by manufacturer, so the only general advice here is: power it off and remove the electrode pads, to avoid harming the rescuer. But this must be done fast, and while waiting for the recharge to finish after removing the pads, you must jump straight back into CPR; the shorter the gap the better, ideally **within 10 seconds** — while still protecting yourself first, since only then can you help the patient.
   - AED and CPR can be alternated until emergency personnel arrive and take over professionally.
5. **Aspirin**

   As in the single-person scenario: first establish whether the patient has a **strong tearing pain**. If the pain is tearing in character, give **neither** **aspirin** **nor** **nitroglycerin**.
   Find aspirin for the patient and, depending on the tablet strength, have them **chew 300 mg** in one go (China's 100 mg version: 3 tablets; US 86 mg version: 4 tablets; 25 mg version: chew 12 tablets).
   If there is none at home and you are in an area with well-developed local commerce, and the patient is not yet showing high-risk features, order it by delivery app. Remember to tip generously and write something like "Patient having an acute heart attack, please deliver as fast as you can, thank you so much" to ask for help, so it arrives as soon as possible.
6. **Lie on the side**

   After taking aspirin, place the patient on the bed or sofa in the **right lateral position** (left arm on top, right arm underneath, legs supported and slightly raised). If no aspirin was taken, keep the semi-recumbent position with the back supported and lower body flat.
7. Prepare items
   - If there are **past medical records** or a **medication list**, find them and have them ready to hand to the medical staff when they arrive;
   - Paper and pen: assuming the patient has not gone into cardiac arrest and there is enough time to monitor their condition before the crew arrives, record the data directly for the crew's reference;
   - Nitroglycerin: it may be needed later; if there is none at home, order it by delivery app as above. Without enough hands on scene, the family member must not leave the patient's side;
   - 1000 ml portable oxygen can: if there is one, find it quickly and put it beside the patient, ready to use if breathing difficulty or low oxygen saturation appears, to ease hypoxia;
   - Blood pressure monitor: have it ready to measure the patient's blood pressure and give the crew valuable reference data;
   - Also, if the home has a pulse oximeter (fingertip type, or a smart watch/band with the same function), a smart watch with ECG capability, or a single-lead ECG device;
   - Ice packs or cooling patches
8. Prepare the environment
   - Indoors: apart from severe northern winters, in all seasons and regions you can open windows to increase ventilation and raise the ambient oxygen level. If the ambient temperature is too high, switch on the air conditioner and set the cooling to its lowest.
   - Outdoors: in hot, humid conditions especially, find ways to lower temperature and humidity — shade during the day, fanning the patient. Cold environments are relatively tolerable; apart from extreme cold, there is no need to worry much.
9. Health monitoring
   - Blood pressure monitoring: this was covered in detail in the single-person chapter; here is a brief summary.
     (1) Measure blood pressure in both arms and both legs — all four limbs (with more people available, you can measure the lower limbs more);
     (2) When measuring **upper-limb blood pressure** the patient should be **semi-recumbent**, with the cuff level with the heart and the cuff's lower edge about 3 cm from the elbow, i.e. roughly the height of an adult's index and middle fingers held together;
     (3) When measuring **lower-limb blood pressure** the patient should be **lying flat**, with the cuff placed above the ankle (2 cm from the ankle, i.e. one adult man's middle-finger width or one adult woman's thumb width);
     (4) Measure the upper limbs first: finish one side completely before switching to the other. For example, if you start with the right arm, take at least 2 or more readings until you have complete, stable data, then switch to the left arm;
     (5) Each side needs **at least two measurements**, at least **1 minute apart**. If two consecutive systolic readings differ by **more than 10 mmHg**, wait at least 1 minute and take a **third** reading; if the difference is still **more than 10 mmHg**, wait at least another minute and take a fourth, a fifth… until **the last two values** are relatively stable, i.e. **less than 10 mmHg** apart;
     (6) After finishing the upper-limb measurements, record them completely. If the difference between the two arms is **more than 10 mmHg**, flag it and tell the medical staff first thing when they arrive;
     (7) Measure the lower limbs using the same method as the upper limbs. Note that lower-limb pressure is normally higher than upper-limb pressure, so do not panic if the **lower-limb** value is **20 or even 40 mmHg higher** than the upper-limb value — just record it calmly;
     (8) If the two lower limbs also differ from each other by **more than 10 mmHg**, do the same: record and flag it, and tell the crew when they arrive;
     (9) Provided the patient is not in danger and there is time, after finishing the above you can calculate one more value — the **ABI (ankle-brachial index)**: of the two upper limbs, take the side with the higher pressure (in a stable state where the last two systolic readings differ by less than 10 mmHg), average those two relatively stable systolic values; then take the blood pressure of the lower limb on the same side, likewise averaging its last two stable systolic values; finally divide the **upper-limb** value by the **lower-limb** value:

     ABI = ((upper-limb sBP reading 1 + upper-limb sBP reading 2)/2) / ((lower-limb sBP reading 1 + lower-limb sBP reading 2)/2)

     If this value is **0.90 or below** or **1.4 or above**, record and flag it, and tell the crew when they arrive;
     (10) If more than 30 minutes pass with no extreme event and the crew has still not arrived, repeat the blood pressure monitoring once, and keep cycling like this until the crew arrives.
   - Measure **oxygen saturation**: as in the single-person chapter, if there is a fingertip pulse oximeter or a smart watch/band, monitor SpO2 continuously; if it stays at **90 or below** and you have something like a portable oxygen can, give oxygen to ease the hypoxia.
   - Watch for **arrhythmia**: if there is a smart watch with ECG monitoring or a portable single-lead ECG device, run a cycle every 5 minutes:
     (1) If something like "premature ventricular contractions" (PVCs) appears, record the total number over the monitoring period;
     (2) If monitoring shows **atrial fibrillation**, **ventricular fibrillation** or **atrial flutter**, record them specifically and tell the crew when they arrive.

> With one more person present, the ambulance crew is still the main force. The family member's most important job is still to **call in more help** and **find more equipment**; to intervene immediately if the patient goes into cardiac arrest; and otherwise to supply the crew with supporting data and information, compressing the time between every medical step to raise the odds of a good outcome.

#### What to do when several people are present

##### With several people present (two or more besides the patient), you still need one person in charge, to coordinate the human resources on scene and avoid chaos. We assume you are that person, acting as the lead.

1. Stabilize the patient: have them lie flat or semi-recumbent (if conscious) in a safe, open and level place, and keep order. If there are too many people, make the crowd spread out — they must not all crowd around the patient. Apart from those providing direct support, everyone else should be pushed back at least 3 meters.
2. At the very same moment, directly designate one person — someone familiar with the area (or at least someone you think may be) and able to speak clearly — to **call 120** and take full responsibility for contacting emergency medical personnel. If necessary (if there are only 2 people besides the patient, they should preferably not leave the scene) they can go to a specific junction or area to meet the ambulance vehicle and guide it to the scene as fast as possible.
3. **CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation)**

   If the patient goes into cardiac arrest, intervene immediately and simultaneously call over anyone else who knows CPR. As noted, CPR is close to anaerobic exercise and extremely draining; one person rarely lasts, so ideally at least 2 people can alternate to manage fatigue. When several people rotate, the handover must be prompt — **the gap must not exceed 10 seconds**.
   The specific steps are not repeated here; refer directly to the two-person chapter.
4. **AED (automated external defibrillator)**

   Whenever there are 3 or more people, all able to move normally, one designated person can go look for a nearby AED — for example, the "liaison" assigned to call 120 can also search for a nearby AED and fetch it quickly. How to find an AED is described in detail in the two-person chapter and is not repeated here.
   If there is enough spare manpower, even after CPR has started you can still look up, point at one or two specific people, and put them in charge of finding a nearby AED. You must designate a specific person. Whether to use it once retrieved, and the basic method, are also described in the two-person chapter — refer directly to that section.
5. **Vulnerable people: the elderly, children, pregnant women**

   If elderly people, children or pregnant women are also on scene, and manpower allows, immediately assign a specific person to help **take them away from the scene**. Someone with empathy and compassion is best; some professional psychological knowledge is even better. On one hand this keeps order — too many people not only disrupt the scene and compete for the precious oxygen in a confined space, but also hit vulnerable people psychologically. As long as there are enough people, assign someone to move them out of sight of the scene. For children, offer **physical embrace** to cushion the psychological shock.
6. Medication or monitoring tools
   If you are outdoors, ask the patient / check whether they carry any medication, and ask whether anyone else has emergency medication (aspirin); for how to use it, refer directly to the two-person chapter.
   If you are indoors, likewise ask the patient / check whether they carry medication, and also ask whether any of the health monitoring tools listed in the two-person chapter exist in the environment. If so, use them as described there.
7. **Keep a record**

   Even when everyone is not a stranger, if there is spare manpower it is best to assign one specific person to video-record the whole process, focusing on the patient and the rescuers, capturing as much of what the rescuers do as possible — for safety and as a record. We now live in a complicated mixed online-and-offline society. "Never harbor the intent to harm others, but never abandon vigilance against being harmed." At critical moments, an objective third-party record can prevent a great deal of trouble.

> With several people present, human resources are relatively abundant, but the overall approach and methods do not differ much from the two-person scenario. The 120 professional emergency crew remains the top priority. More manpower does allow more rotation in CPR, and having someone free to fetch an AED greatly increases the chance of saving the patient. Vulnerable people such as the elderly and children can also be protected and supported psychologically. The one thing to watch: it is best to have a specific person standing by to video-record, which can provide useful protection if trouble arises later.

### Explanatory Notes

Note 1: Remember this above all. When severe discomfort strikes and you suspect an acute myocardial infarction or another cardiac event, you must **never drive yourself, take a taxi, ride a bike, or walk to the hospital**. Call 120 and have them come. Whether you cycle, walk or drive, any of these can increase the load on the heart muscle, and the chance of deteriorating en route is extremely high. If you are at home alone, the single greatest exertion acceptable is getting up to open the door, so that when the 120 crew arrives a locked door does not stop them from getting in to treat you. Beyond that, no movement at all should happen. Do not walk — do not walk — do not walk!

Note 2: Why single out **tearing pain**? This comes back to what we said at the start: cardiac problems are complex, and not everyone who suffers a cardiac arrest is having a myocardial infarction. If the pain has that tearing character, the problem very likely lies in a different direction: **aortic dissection / rupture**. In that case aspirin and nitroglycerin are strictly forbidden; taking medication blindly will only make things worse. Professional handling by the 120 crew plus rapid admission for surgery is virtually the only path to survival. So the character of the pain must be identified clearly — otherwise there may truly be no second chance.

Note 3: Why measure blood pressure, and why measure both arms or even all four limbs? Same reason: a large difference between the two arms also points with high probability to **aortic dissection / rupture**, and again aspirin and nitroglycerin must not be used, and again 120 handling plus admission for surgery is almost the only way to survive.

01. Heart disease (myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest): as modern medicine has advanced, cardiovascular disease has grown into a vast field. This text targets the most common heart (cardiovascular) problem — myocardial infarction, the serious condition caused by plaque rupture and so on blocking a major coronary vessel. The many other detailed classifications, and problems affecting other sub-parts of the heart, are outside this text's scope, though some general approaches are worth borrowing. For example, whatever the situation, getting professional medical resources involved as fast as possible is the core of the core.
02. Calling 110 for help: by standard rules and by conventional morality this method is not entirely commendable. But if the person in front of you is yourself, or your own family, then in the face of a human life I believe that even some moral compromise is a price worth paying. Do you remember the incident where someone blocked an ambulance on the road, phone camera raised, forcing the medical staff to hand over an AED — while being unwilling to break any rule themselves, only applying moral pressure to others? In front of those dearest to you, paying some price is worth it if it is necessary.
03. Human support: when sudden illness strikes, there is very little you can do alone. Even with a family member present, CPR as described demands considerable strength and stamina; one person rarely sustains it for long. So you must find a way to gather more people on scene in the shortest possible time.
04. Young people should be especially careful. With the same sudden acute myocardial infarction, younger people are often more fragile than older ones. Elderly people, having accumulated years of cardiovascular narrowing, may have developed compensation — extra collateral vessel branches that help support blood flow. Relatively young people have not had time to grow those collaterals, so when a high-risk event hits, there may never be a second chance. Compared with the elderly, young and middle-aged people with high-risk factors (hypertension, hyperglycemia, family history, obesity, high BMI, etc.) should pay more attention.
05. The atomized society: the highest-risk group within this is young and middle-aged people living alone in big cities. An overly atomized society can no longer gather a crowd of helpers as quickly as a traditional rural community could; especially in public settings, various social antagonisms have made many people afraid to step in and help. And for someone alone at home, apart from the local police station plus community grid workers and delivery couriers, and the 120 crew still on their way, the only people who can be reliably counted on to arrive at your home quickly and who have institutional backing and the confidence to step in — well, aside from calling 110, there may really be no one else who can help.
06. On aspirin and nitroglycerin: in truth neither nitroglycerin (spray or sublingual tablet) nor aspirin treats an acute myocardial infarction, and neither can prevent cardiac arrest. Nitroglycerin (spray or sublingual tablet) relieves the symptoms of angina, while aspirin guards against the coronary thrombosis and re-infarction caused by platelets aggregating after plaque rupture. You should also know that self-administering aspirin or nitroglycerin before medical care arrives has not yet shown a clear statistical difference in clinical outcomes. This is why **calling 120** must always come **first**: professional medical personnel arriving and delivering professional treatment is the one sure way to raise survival markedly.
07. Lying on the **right side** after taking aspirin: there is currently no sufficient proof for this either; only scattered studies suggest that right-side lying may help absorption slightly through neural regulation. This is just my own habit of thinking in terms of "lean management": improving every last tiny detail to win a larger overall probability. So right-side lying is not a strict method — treat it as reference only. Lying still, however, is strictly applicable.
08. Oxygen: same as above — absent low blood oxygen or breathing difficulty, oxygen has no proven benefit for clinical outcomes either, for now.
09. Opening windows + running the air conditioner cold + ice packs or cooling patches on the head: part of the reason for opening windows is similar to the point on oxygen above — hoping to reduce ambient carbon dioxide and raise oxygen. The other part concerns ambient temperature and controlling the patient's body temperature. Here, the medical community, the guidelines and the various clinical outcome studies are still in dispute. To be clear, the dispute is not about harm; it is that studies disagree on whether mortality drops — some show that lowering body temperature reduces mortality or reduces sequelae afterwards, others show no clear difference. So take it as material for critical consideration.
10. The core of CPR: the main method and precautions were covered in the action manual. What needs adding here is that research shows CPR must be continuous. If people alternate, or if you switch to the AED for defibrillation, the gap in the handover with CPR should ideally be under 10 seconds. To use a loose, not strictly rigorous analogy: it is like stirring a large basin of water — you have to keep stirring hard for the water to move. Once the water has settled back into stillness, getting the current going again means rebuilding it bit by bit from the center. Reperfusion during CPR works the same way: it gets the blood inside the body moving passively again. Once the pause exceeds a certain threshold (10 seconds), another start-up period is needed; the longer the gap, the worse the effect.
11. Protecting vulnerable people: this deserves special explanation. Whether in an open public setting or in a private home full of family, when something like this happens everyone is at a loss. Vulnerable people — children especially — are easily hit hard psychologically in such an environment, and the chaos means many people overlook their special needs. That is why I emphasize in the action manual: if vulnerable people such as children are present, then short of having literally no one to spare, you absolutely must assign someone specifically to protect them and get them away from the environment first. A child may not understand social complexities, but what is written in our genes lets them understand this kind of fear. This must be taken seriously. Never overlook it.
12. CPR training: widely available professional training includes the American Heart Association's **Heartsaver®** course and the **Red Cross first aider certificate**. *[Translator's note: the original text spells the AHA course "Hearthsaver®"; the correct name is Heartsaver®.]* AHA courses are pricier at around RMB 1,000 and valid for 2 years; Red Cross basic rescue training costs RMB 200–300 (international certification RMB 500–600) and is valid for 3 years. Of course, offline learning with hands-on practice works better, but if you just want to study on your own, systematic courses can also be found on Chinese MOOC platforms — for example *First Aid Basics*, *On-Site Life-Saving Knowledge and Skills*, and *Resuscitation Medicine*.

### Recommended Items to Keep at Home

01. Aspirin: common, reliable, effective, cheap. Useful for fever, for inflammation, for rheumatism, and useful for the cardiovascular topic of this piece — crush it into water and it even extends the life of cut flowers. If I could take only one drug beyond Earth into deep space, aspirin would be it.
02. Emergency pill box: for people at high risk, buy a dedicated emergency pill box. The recommended type is the white, clamshell small box shaped like a cigar case: sensibly designed, it holds common emergency medications in separate places and comes with labels for writing expiry dates, so you are not hunting around at the moment of danger.
03. Medical records and medication list: keep these in a fixed, easy-to-reach place too, so that if something happens you can hand them over the moment the crew arrives. I have said this in earlier articles: most people are emotion-driven when something happens; staying calm and expressing yourself clearly and logically in a chaotic environment… well, people who speak clearly even in ordinary, non-emergency situations are rare enough. Simply handing over the records and medication list beats incoherent explanation by ten thousand times.
04. Nitroglycerin: a doctor will prescribe this if it is needed, but as we said above, it is not a treatment and taking it does not prevent a myocardial infarction. Know this clearly.
05. Portable oxygen can (1000 ml): a product you often see in high-altitude tourist areas; not expensive, convenient and practical. I personally suggest keeping two at home.
06. Blood pressure monitor: nothing more to say — every household should have one. I personally prefer a version that runs on AA batteries: universal, and quick to swap. Built-in arrhythmia detection is not a must.
07. Smart band / watch: depends on your habits and budget. With a higher budget and tolerance for a larger body and more frequent charging, choose one with ECG capability for the health angle. The most basic band is fine too, mainly for helping monitor blood oxygen.
08. Fingertip pulse oximeter: if you do not want to wear a watch or band, then in terms of professionalism and the risk stage, the fingertip pulse oximeter really is more suitable. One extra point: a high SpO2 (95 or above) does not mean the heart is fine, but a low SpO2 (90 or below) definitely means something is wrong. Fingertip oximeters are inexpensive, a few dozen yuan. I still suggest keeping one at home, because beyond cardiovascular problems, with the various viral, mycoplasma and chlamydial respiratory infections now common, an oximeter also helps when breathing problems arise — and in those cases a portable oxygen can provides useful support too.
09. Single-lead ECG device: a bit of background. The smart watches with ECG function mentioned earlier, and the portable ECG devices on the market that you hold with both hands to take a simple reading, are all single-lead ECG machines. Such devices can only detect arrhythmias; they cannot judge myocardial ischemia. Everyone should know this. Still, for home use we think having one such device is enough, because what we mainly want to know is whether a myocardial infarction has progressed to something like **atrial fibrillation**; if any of the arrhythmia types mentioned earlier show up, you must be on full alert. Finally, thanks to Chinese manufacturing, cheap versions of these single-lead ECG devices cost only around a hundred yuan; families with high-risk members, I personally suggest buying one.
10. Ice packs / cooling patches: probably already in common use — for everyday fevers, and as a way to protect the brain and body by cooling while waiting for the crew. Worth keeping some.
11. Pen and notepad: we have entered the electronic world, but with smart devices like phones, security has grown stricter; in a real emergency no one can get past your screen lock, and however valuable the information stored in the phone is, it might as well be zero. The more traditional the tool, the more reliable it is.
12. AA batteries: simple devices like the blood pressure monitor and single-lead ECG device mentioned above mostly run on AA batteries. I have real reservations about the trend toward built-in lithium-ion batteries: not only does the battery degrade over time and become hard to replace, but if an emergency strikes — especially in disaster-prone areas — what do you do when the built-in lithium battery is dead?
13. AED (automated external defibrillator): in densely populated areas of big cities, AED coverage is already high. But if you do not live near such places, or if you are relatively well off, buying one for home is now very simple. Again, thanks to Chinese manufacturing: these used to start at RMB 20,000–30,000 and might even be monophasic; now a semi-automatic unit costs under RMB 3,000, and a fully automatic biphasic 360 J unit can be had in the RMB 5,000 range, which makes home ownership possible for far more families and greatly lowers the barrier. The one thing to flag is that an AED also has maintenance costs: both electrode pads and battery need periodic replacement. Pads, depending on their shelf life (3–5 years), may cost RMB 500–1,000 per pair, and the battery (single-use, non-rechargeable) RMB 1,500 and up — that is, maintenance every 5 years costs roughly half the original purchase price of the machine. Whether that trade-off is worth it is for you to judge.

### In Closing

The title is basically fixed from now on: *Suspected Heart Attack and Cardiac Arrest: A Layperson's Field Manual v1.3*. This series is operational-manual-style popular science for sudden emergencies. Separately, an operational manual (SOP) for everyday, general problems is also in preparation as v1.1, again driven by advances in technology.

Compared with strictly medical content, what I add is a set of methods that are more workable in real situations — including some "unorthodox" ones — for ordinary people with limited resources (such as without family support). I hope no one ever needs them. But if you do end up in that situation, I hope you will not be at a loss.
